# Method

## The Genealogical Proof Standard, in short

A conclusion is sound when it meets five tests:

1. **A reasonably exhaustive search.** Look in every source likely to answer the
   question, not just the first that does. One census entry is a start, not a proof.
2. **Complete and accurate citations.** Every fact can be traced to the record it came
   from: what the record is, where it is held, and where in it the entry is.
3. **Analysis and correlation.** Each record is weighed for what it is (below), and the
   records are compared with each other.
4. **Conflicts resolved.** Where records disagree, the reason one is preferred is
   written down, or the conflict is left visible.
5. **A written conclusion.** The reasoning is in the log, so the next researcher can
   follow it.

## Evidence

For each record, ask:

- **Original or derivative?** An image of the register is better than an index of it,
  and an index is better than a tree that copied it. Indexes have transcription errors;
  check against the image when there is one.
- **Who gave the information, and did they know?** A death certificate's date of death
  comes from the time; its date of birth and parents' names come from an informant who
  may have guessed. A census age is often off by a year or two, more for older people.
- **Is it the same person?** Match on more than the name: age, place, family members,
  occupation, and the people around them (neighbours, witnesses, godparents). A record
  that only shares a name is a lead. Common names need at least two independent points
  of agreement.
- **Spellings vary.** Search variants (Carew, Carewe, Carrew, Crewe), first names as
  nicknames or in Latin and Irish forms (Michael, Micheal, Michaelem), and first names
  in place of each other (a middle name used as the first).

## Which records answer which questions

Look for records of every event. The ones that name parents, birthplaces or ages are the
ones that lead backwards.

| Question             | Records to look for                                                                                                                                                                                             |
| -------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Birth date and place | Civil birth registration; baptism (parish register); delayed birth certificate; later records that give age or birthplace: census, marriage, death, military draft, naturalization, Social Security application |
| Parents              | Birth or baptism record; marriage record (often names both fathers); death certificate; obituary; probate (a will naming children); census household                                                            |
| Marriage             | Civil marriage registration; parish marriage register; banns; marriage licence or bond; newspaper announcement; census "years married"                                                                          |
| Death and burial     | Civil death registration; burial register; cemetery or sexton record; gravestone; obituary or death notice; probate                                                                                             |
| Where they lived     | Census; city directory; voter list; land and property records; tax records; church membership                                                                                                                   |
| Immigration          | Passenger list; naturalization (declaration and petition); census "year of immigration"; border crossing; passport application                                                                                  |
| Military service     | Draft registration (US, 1917 to 1918 and 1940 to 1942); service and pension records; muster rolls; war graves                                                                                                   |
| Occupation and life  | Census; city directory; newspaper; trade or union records; court records                                                                                                                                        |

## By place and era

The common cases for this family; ask for others.

**United States**

- Federal census every ten years from 1790; names every household member from 1850;
  relationships to the head from 1880. 1890 was almost all lost. The latest public
  census is 1950.
- State censuses in some states (New York, for one) in the years between.
- Vital records are kept by county or state, starting at different dates: often only
  from the 1880s to 1910s. Before that, use church records, newspapers and probate.
- Social Security Death Index (deaths from about 1962), and SS-5 applications, which
  name parents.
- Naturalization: before 1906 in any court, so look by county; after 1906 more detailed,
  with birth date, birthplace and arrival.

**Ireland**

- Civil registration of births, marriages and deaths from 1864 (Protestant marriages
  from 1845), free online at irishgenealogy.ie.
- Catholic parish registers, mostly from the early to mid 1800s; images free online at
  the National Library of Ireland.
- Censuses: only 1901 and 1911 survive whole, free online at the National Archives of
  Ireland. Earlier ones were mostly destroyed in 1922.
- Griffith's Valuation (1847 to 1864) and the Tithe Applotment Books (1823 to 1837) as
  substitutes, listing householders by townland.

**England and Wales**

- Civil registration from July 1837; the index is free on FreeBMD, and certificates are
  ordered from the General Register Office.
- Census every ten years from 1841 (1921 is the latest public); free transcriptions on
  FreeCEN for some years and counties.
- Parish registers before 1837, held by county record offices.

**Canada**

- Census in 1851 or 1852, then every ten years from 1871 to 1931, free at Library and
  Archives Canada. Vital records are kept by each province.

## When nothing is found

- Widen the dates by five years either side, and drop the place.
- Search for a parent, sibling or child instead, and find the person in their household.
- Search for the surname alone in a small place.
- Consider that the record does not survive, or was never made, and say so in the log.
- A search that found nothing is still worth recording: in the log always, and on the
  page as a `Note:` when it would save the next researcher the same search ("Not found
  in the 1901 Irish census, searched by name and county, 2026").
